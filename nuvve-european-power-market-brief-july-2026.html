European Power Market Brief

A monthly review of European day-ahead and balancing markets — prices, drivers and the summer outlook.

Review June 2026  ·  Outlook July – August 2026

European power markets spent June in their now-familiar summer rhythm: renewables pushing midday and overnight prices toward zero, broken by sharp spikes when the wind faded and the heat built. This issue leads with the Nordic hydro story — the clearest single driver in the European picture right now — then sets it against the continental backdrop of gas, solar and nuclear that will shape July and August.

In brief
  • DK1 day-ahead averaged about €112/MWh in June, with a brief heat-driven spike to €787 intraday on 24 June; the same bimodal pattern — negative midday hours, sharp evening peaks — ran across Northwest Europe.
  • Norwegian reservoir fill recovered to 60.2% from a ~32% April low, but sits 8–12 points below normal — the tightest Nordic hydro setup since 2010 — keeping export headroom thin.
  • Across the Continent, gas (TTF) remains the marginal price-setter in low-renewable hours; a geopolitical gas-supply premium is the main upside tail into summer.
  • The base case (50%) sees July–August DK1 averaging €70–95/MWh, broadly in line with summer 2025. Risk is two-sided.
01June in review

A heat-driven spike against a tight hydro backdrop

Across Northwest Europe the month split into two regimes. When wind and solar were abundant, day-ahead prices fell toward zero or below; when they faded, prices climbed fast. The spread between those states — not the monthly average — is where the value sits, and Denmark, with a wind share above 50%, is the clearest example of the pattern that now also defines Germany and the wider Central-Western Europe (CWE) region.

For most of the month DK1 traded in a familiar band, roughly €46–152/MWh, supported by strong wind and solar. Prices broke higher around 24 June: the daily average reached €211/MWh with an intraday clearing price of €787, as day-ahead load forecasts exceeded available low-cost generation. Heat continued to build, peaking at 33.9°C on 27 June, before temperatures fell to 16–21°C entering July.

The month also underlined Denmark's structural import dependence. June generation averaged 2,070 MW against 2,380 MW of load — a 310 MW deficit covered by imports — while high-wind days (4, 9, 15 June) pushed output to 3,100–4,500 MW and tipped the zone into export mode with near-zero or negative hourly prices.

The backdrop: a stressed but recovering hydro system

Fill entered the year at 69.5% and drew down sharply through an exceptionally dry winter and spring, bottoming near 32% in late April — the steepest Nordic hydro trajectory since 2010. Late snowmelt has since lifted it to 60.2% by week 25, but that still sits 8–12 points under the typical June range of 67–70%. The practical effect reaches well beyond Norway: thinner reservoirs mean less hydro available to export, which firms prices across the DK–SE–NO area and into northern Germany whenever wind is low. It is the conditions behind June's spike, and the single largest variable for the summer.

02Across the European stack

If the Nordic driver is water, the continental one is gas and sun

The Nordic and continental zones are linked through interconnection and a shared merit order, so the same handful of factors set prices across the region — they just take turns leading. Three are worth watching into summer.

Gas still sets the marginal price

Whenever renewables fall short, the most expensive running plant — usually a gas unit — sets the clearing price, which ties European power to the TTF gas benchmark. TTF sat around €35/MWh in late spring, but the summer tail is a geopolitical supply-route premium, estimated at €8–25/MWh on power in escalation scenarios. The pass-through is asymmetric: it barely registers in windy, sunny hours and lands hardest in the low-renewable evening peak, when gas is on the margin.

More solar, more negative hours, wider spreads

The continued build-out of solar across the Continent keeps pushing midday hours toward — and past — zero, with Germany and the Nordics leading the count of negative-price hours. That caps monthly averages but widens the intraday spread, which is the more important number for flexible assets. The pattern is structural now, not seasonal noise.

Nuclear and interconnection set the floor

Summer is the maintenance window for Nordic and continental nuclear; an unplanned Swedish outage (Ringhals or Forsmark, 1–1.4 GW) or extended continental maintenance removes baseload and tightens the regional stack. French nuclear availability remains the swing factor for CWE balance. Because the zones are interconnected, none of this stays local — a squeeze in one bidding zone transmits quickly to its neighbours.

The base case leads because hydro is recovering and medium-range forecasts show no persistent blocking high through mid-July — early July in particular brings strong wind (31–37 m/s on 3–5 July) and cooler air, which should suppress prices and bring back negative hours before the paths diverge. For reference, last summer DK1 averaged €82.8/MWh in July and €73.4 in August, so the base range sits broadly in line with 2025.

Two variables dominate the spread. Hydro trajectory: each 10-point change in fill has historically moved Nordic spot ±8–15%, and a stall below 60% would deepen the supply squeeze. Wind: Denmark's 50%+ wind share splits the distribution — high-wind weeks average €10–30/MWh with frequent negatives, low-wind weeks €120–220. The shared tail across Europe is the gas-price premium described above: in a coincident heat wave, demand couples across Germany, France and the Nordics at the same time wind is weakest, so the upside is correlated rather than zone-by-zone.

Next review trigger. A move in Norwegian fill below 58% during July, or a direct disruption to European gas supply, would shift the forecast upward by an estimated €25–40/MWh. Confidence this edition: medium — hydro and wind both carry wide uncertainty over a 60-day horizon.
04Balancing & dispatch

System stress lifts reserve and arbitrage value

Hydro tightness and wind intermittency move the reserve markets alongside energy. European balancing is increasingly integrated — aFRR is activated through PICASSO, mFRR through MARI, and frequency containment through the FCR cooperation — so stress in one zone lifts reserve value across the region rather than in isolation. As units shift toward energy production during price events, balancing capacity firms across the stack, and the wide intraday swings open repeated arbitrage windows.

The intraday pattern is consistent: charge through high-wind, negative-price windows (typically 02:00–06:00 and 11:00–14:00 CEST) and discharge into the low-wind evening peak (17:00–21:00 CEST). With the system this tight, the value sits with assets that can move between reserve and energy as conditions change rather than committing capacity ahead of a price event.
